Myths and facts

Some common worries stop people from asking for help, or from helping someone else. Here is what the sources on this site say.

Asking someone about suicide does not put the idea in their head

It's a common worry. The National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) says studies show that asking does not increase suicidal thoughts or behavior. What the research shows about asking

Talk of suicide should always be taken seriously

Some people think it's just a bid for attention. NIMH advises taking all talk of suicide seriously. What helps in the conversation

You don't need to be in an emergency to contact 988

The 988 Lifeline says anyone who is struggling can reach out, whether you are depressed, need to talk, or are thinking about suicide. You can also contact it if you're worried about someone. What happens when you reach out to 988

Most 988 calls don't involve emergency services

Some people worry that reaching out means the police will come. The 988 Lifeline reports that fewer than 2 in 100 calls involve emergency services, and that more than half of those happen with the caller's consent. This is not a guarantee. When emergency services get involved

Effective help exists, and recovery is possible

NIMH says effective treatments are available for people at risk of suicide. The American Foundation for Suicide Prevention says recovery is possible with evidence-based treatment for depression and other mental health conditions. What the research shows about treatment

Crisis lines such as 988

Many callers say the call helped

Researchers interviewed 437 adults who had called the Lifeline while having suicidal thoughts. About 7 in 10 said the call helped a lot, and about 1 in 4 said it helped a little.

What it can't show: These are callers' own reports, with no comparison group, from before the 988 number launched. Counselors chose which callers to ask, callers who needed emergency services were not asked, and only people who agreed and could be reached were interviewed. Only English-speaking callers took part, and calls to the Spanish line weren't included. The interviews took place during the COVID-19 pandemic. Some people had suicidal thoughts again later. If that happens, you can call, text, or chat again.

Gould and colleagues, Suicide and Life-Threatening Behavior, 2025

Studies of crisis lines are still limited

A review of 33 studies found only low-strength evidence that crisis lines work. There was some support for people feeling better by the end of a contact, and little good evidence about what happens later. This means there are too few strong studies, not that crisis lines don't help. The authors still concluded that the evidence supports keeping and expanding crisis lines.

What it can't show: The search ended in 2018, before 988, with little evidence on chat and none on text.

Hoffberg and colleagues, Frontiers in Public Health, 2020

Early research since 988 launched

One short study found that suicide deaths among people aged 15 to 34 were lower than a statistical model predicted after 988 began in 2022.

What it can't show: It cannot show that 988 caused this, because other things changed at the same time. Letters in JAMA questioned how deaths were counted and how differences between states were handled, and the authors replied.

Patel and colleagues, JAMA, 2026

Letter from Herring, JAMA, 2026

Letter from Purtle, JAMA, 2026

Reply from the authors, JAMA, 2026

Asking someone about suicide

Asking does not appear to raise suicidal thoughts

A review that pooled 8 randomized trials found no significant increase in suicidal thoughts or behavior, self-harm, or distress among people who were asked about these topics. A 2026 experiment with 700 U.S. teens also found that anonymous suicide questionnaires did not raise the desire to self-injure or to die right afterward.

What it can't show: These studies used questionnaires, interviews, and screening, not conversations between friends or relatives. The teen study measured only immediate effects, and the review's authors asked for more high-quality trials.

Polihronis and colleagues, Archives of Suicide Research, 2022

McGuire and colleagues, Psychiatry Research, 2026

Safety plans

Safety planning is linked to less suicidal behavior in adults

A review of 6 studies in adults found that people who received a safety planning-type intervention had a lower risk of later suicidal behavior than people in comparison groups. It found no significant effect on suicidal thoughts.

What it can't show: Four of the 6 studies were rated at high risk of bias, the pattern of results suggests some less favorable studies may be missing, and no children or teens were included. Some of these programs paired the plan with clinician contact and follow-up calls, so the results don't show what a plan made alone would do. A therapist or other mental health professional can help you make a plan.

Nuij and colleagues, British Journal of Psychiatry, 2021

For children and teens, the evidence is thinner

A review of brief programs built around a safety plan for children and teens found no clear benefit, based on 10 studies, 5 of which could be pooled. Many of these programs also included family sessions or follow-up calls.

What it can't show: The evidence is thin, mostly small studies with moderate to high risk of bias. A benefit has not been shown, and these studies are too small to settle small effects in either direction. The American Academy of Pediatrics says a pediatrician can help a family make a safety plan. You can call or text 988 any time.

Albaum and colleagues, JAMA Pediatrics, 2025, corrected 2026

Staying in touch after a crisis

Follow-up may help after an attempt, but results are mixed

A 2026 review of randomized trials in adults who had attempted suicide found fewer repeat attempts after a mix of brief therapy and follow-up contact. It could not test follow-up contact alone. It also found a small drop in suicidal thoughts, with low certainty, and that drop got weaker with longer follow-up. It found no clear effect on self-harm or on connecting people to care, though few studies looked at those. The evidence on these two results is very uncertain.

A separate 2026 trial with 709 American Indian and Alaska Native adults at high suicide risk added culturally adapted caring messages to help connecting with local resources. People in both groups had fewer suicidal thoughts and attempts over the year, and the messages added no clear benefit. The study can't say why both groups improved.

What it can't show: The review covered programs in treatment and emergency settings. The trial ran in tribal communities and included only English-speaking adults. About a third of participants had no 12-month data. Neither result may apply to other groups or settings. Tidelight does not offer follow-up contact. If you have survived an attempt, the American Foundation for Suicide Prevention has a page called After an attempt. It lists things you can do to support your recovery. You can call or text 988 any time.

Homan and colleagues, eClinicalMedicine, 2026

Comtois and colleagues, JAMA Network Open, 2026

Safer surroundings

Making dangerous items and places harder to reach has support

An overview of 20 research reviews found good support for making dangerous items and places harder to reach across whole populations, with several approaches that appear to work.

What it can't show: These reviews studied laws, sales rules, and structural changes across whole populations, not steps a family takes at home. The strength of the evidence varies by approach, and most of it is observational. Twelve of the 20 reviews got the lowest quality rating, mostly for reporting gaps, and many draw on the same studies. If you're worried about someone, Help Someone has steps you can take.

Steeg and colleagues, BMJ Mental Health, 2025.

Treatment

Some therapies have evidence, with limited certainty

A Cochrane review of 76 trials in adults who self-harm called the evidence for most psychosocial treatments uncertain. It found low-certainty evidence that cognitive behavioral therapy may mean fewer people repeat self-harm at longer follow-up. A review of treatments for young people also called the evidence uncertain overall, with moderate evidence that dialectical behavior therapy reduces suicidal thoughts.

What it can't show: These reviews looked at different ages and outcomes, so they can't be compared directly. Another analysis of 26 trials in teens found that no one therapy worked better than the care the other group received, for repeat self-harm at 12 months. That doesn't mean care doesn't help. A health care provider can talk through what might fit you.

Witt and colleagues, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2021.

Sim and colleagues, JAMA Pediatrics, 2025

Cottrell and colleagues, Health Technology Assessment, 2026

How suicide is talked about

News coverage can matter

A review found that suicides rose on average in the weeks after the media reported a celebrity's death by suicide. This is one reason safe messaging guidance exists. If a death in the news is weighing on you, you can call or text 988 any time.

What it can't show: It found no clear rise after general news reporting on suicide. But only 5 studies looked at that, and only in the days right after the reports. The authors said a link with some kinds of reporting can't be ruled out. Results varied widely, and some studies showing no effect may be unpublished. This kind of study cannot prove cause, and it does not cover talking with someone you care about.

Niederkrotenthaler and colleagues, BMJ, 2020.

Stories of coping may help a little

When researchers combined several controlled trials, stories of people recovering from a suicidal crisis slightly reduced suicidal thoughts in people already at some risk.

What it can't show: The effect is small, and it was measured only in the short term, from right after the story to 4 weeks later. The trials measured suicidal thoughts, not suicidal behavior. All were done in Australia or Austria, by members of the review's own team. There was not enough evidence to say whether these stories change attitudes toward seeking help.

Niederkrotenthaler and colleagues, The Lancet Public Health, 2022

What this page can't tell you

Research describes groups of people. It can't predict what will happen for any one person.

No study has tested Tidelight, and nothing here shows that this website prevents suicide. In one trial on a Dutch helpline's website, a short message did not increase use of the helpline link. People who saw it did rate themselves as more likely to contact the helpline later, but the study could not check whether they did (van der Burgt and colleagues, JMIR Mental Health, 2024). Evidence specific to minors is thin.

Thin evidence usually means there aren't enough strong studies yet. It doesn't mean help doesn't work, or that nothing can help you. The American Foundation for Suicide Prevention says recovery is possible with evidence-based treatment. You can call or text 988 any time.

About Tidelight

Tidelight is an early project to help people in the United States find suicide prevention support. Tidelight is not a crisis service. The call, text, and chat links on this site connect you to outside services, such as the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. If you or someone else is in immediate danger, call 911.

Privacy: this site has no sign-ups, forms, analytics, or cookies. It is hosted on GitHub Pages, and GitHub says it logs and stores visitors' IP addresses for security. Quick exit and saved offline copies have limits. Read about privacy and what this site can't hide.
