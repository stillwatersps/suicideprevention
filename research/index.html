What the research shows

Studies can show what tends to help, and where the evidence is still thin. Every finding below links to its source.

The 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline is free, confidential, and available any time. It is a separate service from Stillwater. You don't need research to reach out.

On a device that can't call or text? Call or text 988 from a phone, or go to 988lifeline.org. In immediate danger, call 911.

Crisis lines such as 988

Many callers say the call helped

Researchers interviewed 437 adults who had called the Lifeline while having suicidal thoughts. About 98 in 100 said the call helped.

What it can't show: These are callers' own reports, with no comparison group, from before the 988 number launched. About 4 in 10 said they had suicidal thoughts again after the call.

Gould and colleagues, Suicide and Life-Threatening Behavior, 2025

Strong proof is still limited

A review of 33 studies found only low-strength evidence that crisis lines work. There was some support for people feeling better by the end of a contact, and little good evidence about what happens later.

What it can't show: The search ended in 2018, before 988, with little evidence on chat and none on text.

Hoffberg and colleagues, Frontiers in Public Health, 2020

Early research since 988 launched

One short study found that suicide deaths among people aged 15 to 34 were lower than a statistical model predicted after 988 began in 2022.

What it can't show: It cannot show that 988 caused this, because other things changed at the same time. Other researchers have raised questions about its methods.

Patel and colleagues, JAMA, 2026

Asking someone about suicide

Asking does not appear to raise suicidal thoughts

A review that pooled 8 randomized trials found no significant increase in suicidal behavior, self-harm, or distress among people who were asked about these topics. A 2026 experiment with 700 U.S. teens also found that anonymous suicide questionnaires did not raise the desire to self-injure or to die right afterward.

What it can't show: These studies used questionnaires, interviews, and screening, not conversations between friends or relatives. The teen study measured only immediate effects, and the review's authors asked for more high-quality trials.

Polihronis and colleagues, Archives of Suicide Research, 2022

McGuire and colleagues, Psychiatry Research, 2026

Safety plans

Safety planning is linked to less suicidal behavior

A review of 6 studies found that people who received a safety planning-type intervention had a lower risk of later suicidal behavior than people in comparison groups. It found no significant effect on suicidal thoughts.

What it can't show: This is a small number of studies. Some of these programs paired the plan with clinician contact and follow-up calls, so the results don't show what a plan made alone would do.

Nuij and colleagues, British Journal of Psychiatry, 2021

For children and teens, the evidence is thinner

A review of safety planning on its own for children and teens found no clear benefit, based on a few studies.

What it can't show: The evidence is thin. This means a benefit has not been shown, not that safety plans are harmful.

Albaum and colleagues, JAMA Pediatrics, 2025, corrected 2026

Staying in touch after a crisis

Follow-up may help after an attempt, but results are mixed

A 2026 review of randomized trials in adults who had attempted suicide found that brief interventions and follow-up contacts lowered repeat attempts. A separate 2026 trial with 709 American Indian and Alaska Native adults found that adding caring messages to enhanced usual care did not improve outcomes more than enhanced usual care alone.

What it can't show: These are clinical programs, and they may not apply to other settings. Stillwater does not offer follow-up contact.

Homan and colleagues, eClinicalMedicine, 2026

Comtois and colleagues, JAMA Network Open, 2026

Safer surroundings

Limiting access to dangerous means is a supported strategy

An overview of 20 systematic reviews concluded that limiting access to the means of suicide is well supported as a prevention strategy, with several approaches appearing effective.

What it can't show: The strength of the evidence varies by approach. Most of it is observational, and many of the reviews were rated low quality.

Steeg and colleagues, BMJ Mental Health, 2025

Treatment

Some therapies have evidence, with limited certainty

A Cochrane review of 76 trials in adults who self-harm called the evidence for most psychosocial treatments uncertain. It found low-certainty evidence that cognitive behavioral therapy may mean fewer people repeat self-harm at longer follow-up. A review of treatments for young people also called the evidence uncertain overall, with moderate evidence that dialectical behavior therapy reduces suicidal thoughts.

What it can't show: The research disagrees in places. Another analysis of 26 trials in adolescents found no evidence that any therapy beat comparison care for repeat self-harm at 12 months. A health care provider can talk through what might fit you.

Witt and colleagues, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2021

Sim and colleagues, JAMA Pediatrics, 2025

Cottrell and colleagues, Health Technology Assessment, 2026

How suicide is talked about

News coverage can matter

A review found that suicides rose on average in the weeks after the media reported a celebrity's death by suicide. This is one reason safe messaging guidance exists.

What it can't show: This kind of study cannot prove cause. It is about news coverage of celebrities, not about talking with someone you care about.

Niederkrotenthaler and colleagues, BMJ, 2020

Stories of coping may help a little

Pooled randomized trials found that stories of people recovering from a suicidal crisis slightly reduced suicidal thoughts among people with some vulnerability to suicide.

What it can't show: The effect is small, and there was not enough evidence to say whether these stories change attitudes toward seeking help.

Niederkrotenthaler and colleagues, The Lancet Public Health, 2022

What this page can't tell you

Research describes groups of people. It can't predict what will happen for any one person.

No study has tested Stillwater, and nothing here shows that this website prevents suicide. In one trial on a Dutch helpline's website, a short message did not increase use of the helpline link (van der Burgt and colleagues, JMIR Mental Health, 2024). Evidence specific to minors is thin.

About Stillwater

Stillwater is an early project to help people in the United States find suicide prevention support. Stillwater is not a crisis service. The call, text, and chat links on this site connect you to outside services, such as the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. If you or someone else is in immediate danger, call 911.
