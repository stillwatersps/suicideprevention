After a suicide attempt

If you survived a suicide attempt, or someone you love did, you are not alone. Many people have been where you are and found their way forward. Here is what can help in the days and weeks after, and where to find more support.

Need to talk to someone now?

  • Call 988Talk to a counselor
  • Text 988Send any message
  • Chat OnlineWeb chat with 988

988 says a text starts with a few steps. Reply NEXT to accept its terms, answer a brief survey, and wait for a counselor.

The 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline is free, confidential, and available any time. It is a separate service from Tidelight.

On a device that can't call or text? Call or text 988 from a phone, or go to 988lifeline.org. In immediate danger, call 911.

If you survived an attempt

Your life matters. The American Foundation for Suicide Prevention (AFSP) says many people have been on this path of healing and survived it.

It's okay if you don't understand all the thoughts and feelings that led to this. AFSP says many people who feel this way are living with a mental health condition that can be treated.

The days and weeks after

AFSP says you deserve to take the time you need. It suggests that:

  • Rest, regular meals, exercise, and time with people who support you can all help your mood and health.
  • It's okay to ask people you trust to take over some daily tasks for a while.
  • When you're ready, you can tell people you trust what happened, and ask them to help you stay safe.

Care and follow-up

AFSP says a good therapist or doctor can help you make sense of what happened. A mental health professional can also help you make a safety plan and deal with stress that is still going on.

Go to follow-up appointments if you can, and tell your doctor or therapist if thoughts of suicide come back. The time after leaving a hospital is when support matters most. The Suicide Prevention Resource Center (SPRC) says risk stays higher for months after a hospital stay. AFSP says the first six months are especially important, and that risk stays higher for the whole first year. The National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) says staying in touch with people at risk, and following up with them, has been linked to fewer later attempts.

If you don't have care yet, you can ask a doctor for a referral. SAMHSA, a U.S. government health agency, describes FindTreatment.gov as a confidential and anonymous way to look for treatment near you. See what the research shows about staying in touch after a crisis.

A plan for hard moments

A safety plan is a short written list of what to do, and who to reach, if a crisis starts again. AFSP says it usually covers:

  • What puts you at risk
  • Ways to cope on your own
  • People and places that take your mind off things
  • Family or friends who can help
  • Calling or texting 988
  • Keeping your surroundings safe

Make a safety plan, with a blank plan to print.

Talking with others

If people ask about your attempt, AFSP says you can tell them what you're comfortable sharing, or that you need time. Some people may react in ways that don't help. AFSP says you aren't responsible for how they work through their own feelings.

AFSP also says a support group can help you know you are not alone. There are groups for people who have survived an attempt, and for depression and other mental health conditions.

If you're under 18, it can help to tell an adult you trust. You can also call or text 988 yourself. More for teens.

If someone you love survived an attempt

AFSP says you may feel as if you've been through a trauma yourself, and that it's important to get support and look after yourself too. Its advice for families and friends includes:

  • Be there. Even when you don't know what to say, being with them helps. AFSP says that in the first few weeks, they need you very close.
  • Talk openly, and ask what they need. Encourage them to talk with their therapist or counselor about a safety plan.
  • Support their care. Encourage them to stay in counseling, and to tell their care team about any thoughts of suicide.
  • Help make home safer. Ask their care team how you can help make their surroundings safer.
  • Support everyday well-being, such as sleep, regular meals, exercise, and things they find relaxing.
  • Be patient and gentle. AFSP says recovery usually goes on long after a hospital stay.

The 988 Lifeline says you can contact 988 at any time if someone you know is struggling, and that helpers should reach out for support themselves. More on helping someone.

About Tidelight

Tidelight is an early project to help people in the United States find suicide prevention support. Tidelight is not a crisis service. The call, text, and chat links on this site connect you to outside services, such as the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. If you or someone else is in immediate danger, call 911.

Privacy: this site has no sign-ups, forms, analytics, or cookies. It is hosted on GitHub Pages, and GitHub says it logs and stores visitors' IP addresses for security. Quick exit and saved offline copies have limits. Read about privacy and what this site can't hide.
