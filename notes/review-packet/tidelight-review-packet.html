How to use this packet

What's inside:

  • Part 1: Text that appears on every page
  • Part 2: Each English page, sentence by sentence, with its source links
  • Part 3: Wording outside the page text, such as what a screen reader says and the contact card
  • Part 4: The founder-supplied research brief
  • Part 5: Crisis resources, and what each button does
  • Part 6: Questions written for you
  • Part 7: How the research was read, and how the pages compare with the brief
  • Part 8: Proposed shorter sentences, optional
  • Part 9: Decisions and sign-off

The Spanish pages are in a separate packet for a fluent reader. The Spanish lines on the English pages, such as the Spanish part of Get Help, appear here as part of each page. The fluent reader also checks their wording.

How to record feedback:

  • Mark each section. Each section ends with a box: OK as written, OK with changes, or Not for the website.
  • Refer to sentences by number. Every sentence has a number, such as HELP-12. In the Notes line, write something like "HELP-12: change to ...". Writing on the page is fine.
  • Repeated sentences. A gray sentence marked "same as" repeats an earlier one. A decision on the first applies to all of them.
  • Answer the questions in Part 6 if you can. Skip any that aren't yours to answer.
  • Finish with Part 9. It records your decisions, your role, and the date. Please don't write personal contact details in this packet. It may be stored in a public project.

You can use a pen on paper, or add notes to the PDF in a PDF app. Return it to the founder who gave it to you.

If anything here could cause harm, please say so first. Use this box, or tell the founders right away, for anything that should come down or change before anything else happens.

You can see the draft website on a screen if you meet a founder in person. Otherwise this packet is how to read it. The draft pages are not on the public website yet. Parts of an older version are public at https://stillwatersps.github.io/suicideprevention/.

BRIEF-6 1. Understanding Suicide and Suicidal Behavior

BRIEF-7 Definitions.

BRIEF-8 Suicide is a death resulting from intentional self-directed behavior.

BRIEF-9 Suicidal ideation means thoughts about suicide; a suicide attempt is nonfatal suicidal behavior.

BRIEF-10 Suicide prevention includes interventions that reduce risk and strengthen access to care.

BRIEF-11 Public health perspective.

BRIEF-12 Suicide is influenced by overlapping individual, interpersonal, community and societal factors.

BRIEF-13 No one diagnosis or experience explains every case.

BRIEF-14 Myths and accurate framing.

BRIEF-15 Depression is one risk factor, not a universal cause.

BRIEF-16 Asking someone directly and compassionately about suicide does not implant the idea.

BRIEF-17 Prevention and recovery are possible.

BRIEF-18 Use nonjudgmental language such as "died by suicide."

BRIEF-19 2. Causes and Risk Factors

BRIEF-20 Psychological factors.

BRIEF-21 Depression, anxiety, trauma-related symptoms, substance use disorders and other mental health difficulties may contribute to vulnerability.

BRIEF-22 A diagnosis alone does not predict an individual outcome.

BRIEF-23 Biological factors.

BRIEF-24 Research investigates genetic vulnerability, stress-response systems, sleep disturbance and emotional regulation.

BRIEF-25 None is a deterministic explanation.

BRIEF-26 Social and environmental factors.

BRIEF-27 Bullying, discrimination, interpersonal conflict, loss, economic instability, violence, social isolation and limited access to care can add stress.

BRIEF-28 Risk factors can accumulate or interact.

BRIEF-29 Protective factors.

BRIEF-30 Supportive relationships, belonging, accessible treatment, coping skills, stable environments and continuity of care can reduce risk.

BRIEF-31 Protective factors help but do not guarantee safety.

Research

  • Are the findings fair? Each has a "What it can't show" line. Please check every number against its source.
  • Is it right to include the study on news coverage of celebrity deaths? It is worded without figures and says it does not apply to talking with someone.
  • Three papers are cited without links because they name methods. Should they be linked? Open these knowing that: Steeg 2025, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12699607/, the BMJ 2020 review, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7190013/, and Witt 2021, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8094743/, whose full text names and ranks methods. The pages no longer say why they aren't linked, because that line told readers where method details are. Is a citation with no link and no reason clear enough? The other research and source links were not all checked for this.
  • The page dropped a figure: about 4 in 10 interviewed callers had suicidal thoughts again by the interview, about two weeks later on average (Gould 2025, 40.8%). A figure with no step can read as "calling doesn't last", so the page says "Some people had suicidal thoughts again later" and says they can reach out again. Was that right?
  • Albaum 2025: the printed result for suicidal thoughts (Hedges g 0.11, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.21) excludes zero and sits on the side favoring the comparison group, while the authors call it not significant. Figure 2A shows the same. The page says a benefit has not been shown and that the studies can't settle small effects in either direction. Please read the figure, and consider asking the authors.
  • Steeg 2025: evidence is weak for one policy area studied mainly in the U.S. The page only says strength varies by approach. Should it say more, for a U.S. site?
  • Studies of messages alone are mixed, including Witt 2021, Skopp 2023, and Reger 2024 and 2026. The page gives one such trial (Comtois). Should it say more?
  • Evidence moved recently: the caring messages trial was published November 2, 2026, and the 988 mortality paper has published letters and a reply. Recheck before release.
  • A commentary published with the caring messages trial (PMID 42970295, read only through the summary tool) raises low power and a scale validated in younger people, and calls it premature to conclude that the messages don't work. Should the limit line say so?
  • Witt 2021 gives higher certainty ratings to two other therapies, each resting on one or two trials, and the authors call for more study. The page names only cognitive behavioral therapy, whose effect wasn't seen at the end of treatment. Should it say more?
  • The myth "Effective help exists, and recovery is possible" links to a section that calls the evidence uncertain. That section's heading and limit line explain why, but is the link's wording enough of a bridge?
  • A large trial published October 28, 2026 (PMID 42946687) compared calls and two-way texts after a suicidal crisis, with 723 teens and 797 adults, on top of usual care, with no group that went without this follow-up. It isn't on the page, and nothing on the page is wrong because of it. Should it be added at the recheck?
  • A letter and an authors' reply exist on the adult safety planning review (PMID 35354503). Nobody has read them, because the journal blocked the check. A person should open them.
  • Gould 2025 was funded through a SAMHSA grant by way of a Vibrant contract, one author is affiliated with Vibrant, callers were paid $50 for the interview, and the authors declare no conflicts. Should the limit line say so, for a study of a service this site recommends?
