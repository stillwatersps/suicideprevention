## Full text of the brief

### Suicide Prevention Research & Website Content

Research reference and implementation brief | Prepared November 2026

Purpose: Educational background for a suicide-prevention website. This material
is not a diagnostic tool or substitute for professional mental health care.
Statistics are year-specific and should be rechecked before publication.

### 1. Understanding Suicide and Suicidal Behavior

**Definitions.** Suicide is a death resulting from intentional self-directed
behavior. Suicidal ideation means thoughts about suicide; a suicide attempt is
nonfatal suicidal behavior. Suicide prevention includes interventions that reduce
risk and strengthen access to care.

**Public health perspective.** Suicide is influenced by overlapping individual,
interpersonal, community and societal factors. No one diagnosis or experience
explains every case.

**Myths and accurate framing.** Depression is one risk factor, not a universal
cause. Asking someone directly and compassionately about suicide does not implant
the idea. Prevention and recovery are possible. Use nonjudgmental language such
as "died by suicide."

### 2. Causes and Risk Factors

**Psychological factors.** Depression, anxiety, trauma-related symptoms, substance
use disorders and other mental health difficulties may contribute to
vulnerability. A diagnosis alone does not predict an individual outcome.

**Biological factors.** Research investigates genetic vulnerability,
stress-response systems, sleep disturbance and emotional regulation. None is a
deterministic explanation.

**Social and environmental factors.** Bullying, discrimination, interpersonal
conflict, loss, economic instability, violence, social isolation and limited
access to care can add stress. Risk factors can accumulate or interact.

**Protective factors.** Supportive relationships, belonging, accessible
treatment, coping skills, stable environments and continuity of care can reduce
risk. Protective factors help but do not guarantee safety.

### 3. Warning Signs and Recognizing Distress

**Emotional and behavioral indicators.** Potential concerns include persistent
hopelessness, feeling trapped, intense shame, withdrawal, significant changes in
sleep or functioning, agitation, and direct statements about suicide. Warning
signs are not a diagnosis.

**Responding.** Listen calmly and without judgment. Ask clearly about suicide
when concerned. Do not dismiss statements as attention-seeking or promise secrecy
where safety is at risk. Involve qualified help and a trusted adult for young
people.

**Urgent support.** In the United States, call or text 988 for crisis support.
For immediate life-threatening danger, contact emergency services. A website
should make these pathways easy to find.

### 4. Effects on Families, Friends, Schools and Communities

**Family and peer impact.** Suicide bereavement may involve grief, anxiety,
confusion, anger, stigma and unanswered questions. Survivors are not responsible
for another person's death.

**Schools and communities.** Schools may need coordinated counseling, careful
communication, support for staff and students, and referral pathways.
Postvention means organized support after a suicide.

**Responsible reporting.** Avoid sensationalism and simplistic explanations.
Communicate the availability of support and recovery; avoid framing suicide as
inevitable.

### 5. Evidence-Based Prevention

**Three levels.** Primary prevention reduces upstream risks through
connectedness, education and supportive environments. Secondary prevention
recognizes emerging concerns and connects people to care. Tertiary prevention
emphasizes recovery, follow-up and continuity of treatment.

**Community approaches.** Strengthen access to affordable mental healthcare,
address bullying and violence, promote social support, reduce harmful barriers
and create clear referral pathways.

**WHO LIVE LIFE framework.** WHO recommends a coordinated public health approach
including responsible media communication, adolescent life skills, early
identification and support, and safer environments.

**Evaluation.** Track access to support, user comprehension, referral
completion, privacy and accessibility. Website engagement alone is not evidence
of suicide prevention efficacy.

### 6. Treatment and Recovery

**Professional care.** Treatment may include cognitive behavioral therapy,
dialectical behavior therapy, family or group therapy, psychiatric evaluation,
and management of underlying conditions. Selection depends on individual needs
and qualified clinical assessment.

**Safety planning.** A collaborative safety plan may identify personal warning
signs, coping approaches, trusted contacts, professional support and steps to
maintain safety. It is not a substitute for treatment.

**Continuity.** Recovery is not necessarily linear. Follow-up, supportive
relationships and adjusted care plans can help people manage renewed distress.

### 7. Statistics and Research Interpretation

**Global context.** WHO has reported more than 720,000 suicide deaths worldwide
annually based on 2021 estimates. Figures change with updated data and methods;
check the latest WHO fact sheet before publishing exact figures.

**United States context.** CDC surveillance reports tens of thousands of suicide
deaths annually, with substantially larger numbers reporting suicidal thoughts.
Verify the specific year and source for all numerical claims.

**Interpretation.** Do not mix deaths, thoughts, attempts or age groups. Survey
methods, reporting completeness and data years differ. Population associations
do not predict an individual person's behavior.

### 8. Technology, Social Media and Website Design

**Digital opportunities.** A website can provide clear education, resource
directories, accessible support navigation and links to professional help.

**Digital risks.** Misinformation, privacy problems, inaccessible interfaces,
harmful content and overconfident automated advice can undermine trust and
safety.

**Design principles.** Use calm, readable typography, plain language, mobile
accessibility, strong contrast, visible human support options and minimal
collection of sensitive data.

**AI boundaries.** AI can help organize educational content but should never
claim to diagnose, replace a therapist or serve as the only crisis response.
Have clinicians review crisis-related flows.

### Website Copy and Agent Implementation Brief

These are three distinct visitor pathways. Keep language gentle,
nonjudgmental, and brief. The first two are supportive reflection tools, not
clinical assessments.

#### Pathway A — Navigate Your Thoughts

Heading: Not sure what you're feeling? Start here.

Website-ready copy: You don't need to have the right words. Take a moment to
notice what is on your mind. You can explore your feelings at your own pace,
without pressure or judgment. If you need support, there are people ready to
listen.

- Optional prompt: "What feeling is strongest right now?" with options such as
  overwhelmed, sad, anxious, numb, unsure.
- Offer a gentle check-in and the choice to pause, skip or speak with someone.
- Provide a visible "Get support now" link throughout.

#### Pathway B — Work Through Your Thoughts

Heading: A little space to reflect, one step at a time.

Website-ready copy: When thoughts feel overwhelming, it may help to slow down and
focus on what you need in this moment. Try naming what is difficult, what might
help you feel supported, and one small, safe next step. You do not have to
handle everything alone.

- Invite optional journaling: "What has been weighing on you?"
- Offer grounding choices such as noticing surroundings, taking a break, or
  reaching out to a trusted person.
- End with a choice to save privately, clear the entry, or view support
  resources.

#### Pathway C — Learn About Suicide

Heading: Understand the facts. Recognize the signs. Find ways to help.

Website-ready copy: Suicide is a complex public health issue influenced by mental
health, life experiences, relationships, and access to support. Learning about
warning signs, risk factors, prevention, and treatment can help reduce stigma and
encourage compassionate conversations. Support and recovery are possible.

- Create short, clearly titled educational modules: Understanding Suicide; Risk
  and Protective Factors; Warning Signs; Prevention; Treatment; Helping Someone
  Else.
- Cite WHO, CDC, NIMH and SAMHSA; show last-reviewed dates.
- Keep crisis support visible and avoid graphic or sensationalized content.

#### Universal safety and product requirements

- Show 988 (U.S. call/text) and an emergency-services pathway in a consistently
  visible location; adapt resources by visitor region.
- Do not require registration, payment or completion of a questionnaire to
  access support.
- Make all reflective questions optional. Provide clear Skip, Back, Exit and
  Clear options.
- Do not present automated risk scores as diagnosis, or imply the website is
  monitored by live clinicians unless it truly is.
- Avoid collecting sensitive journal text by default; explain retention,
  deletion and confidentiality limitations plainly.
- Have qualified mental health professionals review wording, crisis escalation
  pathways and updates before launch.
