Getting help for yourself

If you're struggling or having thoughts of suicide, you don't have to handle it alone. Here are ways to reach someone and what to expect.

What to say when you call or text 988

You don't need special words. 988 says you can start a text with any message. Once you're connected, the counselor speaks first: 988 says they say hello, introduce themselves, and ask if you are safe.

If it helps to have words ready, you could start with one of these. They are Tidelight's examples, not 988's, based on who 988 says can reach out: anyone who is struggling, needs to talk, or is thinking about suicide, and anyone worried about a friend.

  • “I just need someone to talk to.”
  • “I'm having a really hard time.”
  • “I'm thinking about suicide.”
  • “I'm worried about someone I know.”
  • “I don't know what to say, but I need to talk.”

After asking about your safety, 988 says the counselor listens, tries to understand how things are affecting you, offers support, and may share resources. On a text, automated steps come first: reply NEXT to accept 988's terms, answer a brief survey, and then you get a message asking you to wait while 988 connects you to a counselor.

What happens when you reach out to 988

You don't need to be sure it's an emergency. The 988 Lifeline says anyone who is struggling can reach out, whether you are depressed, need to talk, or are thinking about suicide. It also takes concerns about alcohol or drug use.

Show moreShow less: What happens when you reach out to 988

If you call

You'll hear a short recorded message with options, such as pressing 1 for the Veterans Crisis Line, 2 for Spanish, or 3 for LGBTQ+ youth. You don't have to press anything. If you stay on the line, you'll be connected to a local 988 center, based on your approximate location or your phone's area code. SAMHSA, a federal agency that funds 988, says that if that center can't take your call, it goes to a national backup center. Hold music plays while you are connected. SAMHSA says wait times vary. If your wait is longer, it asks you to stay on the line. A counselor then says hello, asks if you are safe, listens to what's going on, and shares resources that may help.

If you text

Send any message to 988. Automated messages come first. Reply NEXT to accept the terms of service, then answer a few questions that help the counselor know how to support you. Next comes a message asking you to wait while 988 connects you to a counselor. The counselor says hello, asks if you are safe, and listens.

If you chat online

The chat opens with a short form that helps the counselor know how to support you. It asks for your zip code, whether you are having thoughts of suicide, and how upset you feel. A phone number is optional. Starting the chat accepts the terms of service. You then wait to be connected to a counselor.

What it costs

Contacting 988 is free, and you don't need insurance. Your phone carrier may still charge for calls or texts if your plan doesn't include unlimited use. If a counselor contacts emergency services, such as 911 or a mobile crisis team, you may get a bill. This could include an ambulance or hospital care. Services they refer you to may charge too, so ask about fees first. The Lifeline's terms say a small number of its centers bill Medicaid or other health insurance and may ask for your personal information. They must explain why. You can say no and still get support. If you give it and a claim is filed, you, a parent or guardian, or the person you called about may get an insurance statement.

If you can't get through

Try another way. If a call doesn't connect, try text or chat. If texting is busy, try a call or chat. In an emergency, call 911.

What stays private

The 988 Lifeline says conversations are confidential, but contacting it isn't anonymous. You don't have to give your name or your exact location to get support. The counselor has your phone number if you call or text, or your IP address if you chat. In rare emergencies they must give this to 911, and 911 may ask your phone or internet company for your exact location.

Counselors take notes, and centers keep records for at least three years. Call recordings or chat and text transcripts may be used to improve the service. You can ask a counselor not to share your personal information. The Lifeline's confidentiality statement lists when information can be shared, including when someone is at imminent risk of harm, when the law requires it, or when you agree.

On a shared phone or computer, visits to this site can show up in browser history, and calls or texts can show up in phone logs. On a school or work device or account, the school or employer may see what you search and read, even in a private window. Whether a call appears on a phone bill depends on the carrier. When emergency services get involved

The 988 Lifeline is separate from 911, and SAMHSA says most crises are managed by counselors working with you, without involving law enforcement. The Lifeline recommends that counselors contact emergency services, which can mean 911, police, or a sheriff, only if someone is in danger of being hurt right now or very soon and they can't work out a safer plan together. It reports that fewer than 2 in 100 calls involve emergency services, and that more than half of those happen with the caller's consent.

This is a recommendation, not a guarantee. The Lifeline's terms say that if a counselor believes there is an immediate threat to someone's physical safety, you may not get a say: they may ask for 911 or a mobile crisis team to come to your location, and can share your information without your permission. If you need help because of an immediate threat to your safety, the terms ask you to call 911 first.

These details come from the 988 Lifeline's website and SAMHSA, and can change. Tidelight does not run 988 and cannot see who contacts it.

  • Veterans Crisis Line

    For veterans, service members, National Guard and Reserve members, and the people who support them. Free, confidential, and available 24/7. You don't need to be enrolled in VA benefits or health care.

  • The Trevor Project

    For LGBTQ+ young people. Its terms describe the phone lifeline as for people under age 25. Trained counselors by phone, text, or chat, free and confidential, 24/7. Message and data rates may apply to texts. On a phone, The Trevor Project suggests calling or texting, and using chat from a computer. Its terms say phone calls offer access to interpreters, subject to availability. You can choose the language option on the keypad or ask the counselor for an interpreter.

    Call 1-866-488-7386 Text START to 678-678 Trevor Project chat and TTY

  • Crisis Text Line

    Trained volunteer crisis counselors by text, free and confidential, 24/7, anywhere in the United States. Crisis Text Line says your first message can say anything, and a word like HOME helps it know how you found it. Its FAQ says messages are free on AT&T, T-Mobile, Sprint, and Verizon. On other carriers, it says standard rates apply and 741741 will appear on the bill. Its terms say message and data rates may apply. Crisis Text Line says many T-Mobile prepaid plans don't allow numbers like 741741. It also says it isn't available yet on Google Voice or TextNow. If a text doesn't go through, it offers web chat and WhatsApp on its website. Its privacy policy says WhatsApp may also have access to your information. You can also call or text 988.

    Text HOME to 741741 Crisis Text Line website

  • 911

    If you or someone else is in immediate danger, call 911. Texting 911 only works in some places, and the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) advises making a voice call if you can. If you text 911 where it isn't available, you should get an automatic message telling you to use another way. If you are Deaf, hard of hearing, or can't speak and texting 911 isn't available, use a TTY or relay service if you can.

    Call 911

The 988 Lifeline, the Veterans Crisis Line, The Trevor Project, and Crisis Text Line keep conversations confidential, with limits. Each says it may contact emergency services or other authorities in some situations. Their websites explain when.

Lines for specific groups

Some lines serve particular groups: people who are pregnant or just had a baby, people affected by a disaster, teens, and trans and questioning people. Each is a separate service with its own rules, and Tidelight does not run them. Teen Line and Trans Lifeline keep set hours. 988 is open any time, and in immediate danger, call 911.

Show moreShow less: Lines for specific groups

  • National Maternal Mental Health Hotline

    For people who are pregnant or just had a baby. Partners and family members are encouraged to contact it too. The Health Resources and Services Administration (HRSA), a federal agency, says it is free, confidential, and available 24/7 in English and Spanish, by call, text, or chat. Trained counselors listen, connect you with local support groups, and refer you to other health care professionals if you need more care. Interpreters are offered for over 60 languages, and you can use TTY or another relay service. HRSA's pages don't call it a crisis line or say what to do in an emergency.

    Call 1-833-852-6262 Text 1-833-852-6262 Hotline page, with its chat

  • Disaster Distress Helpline

    For anyone in the U.S. and its territories in emotional distress related to a natural or human-caused disaster. That includes survivors, loved ones of victims, first responders, rescue and relief workers, clergy, and parents and caregivers. You can reach out for yourself or for someone else. SAMHSA calls it a crisis support service, open 24/7, with trained counselors from crisis centers across the country. They listen, share coping tips, and can refer you to a local crisis center for follow-up. You don't have to give identifying information. For Spanish, call and press 2. For American Sign Language, call or text through your preferred relay provider. Counselors can add an interpreter for over 100 languages, though a less common language may mean calling back at a set time. Standard text and data rates apply to texts.

    Call 1-800-985-5990 Text 1-800-985-5990 Disaster Distress Helpline page

  • Teen Line

    For young people up to age 19. Trained teen volunteers answer: high school students from the Los Angeles area, supervised by adult mental health professionals. Teen Line is a program of Didi Hirsch Mental Health Services. It is free, and only in English. It isn't open all day and night. You can call every night from 6 to 10 p.m. Pacific time, or text from 6 to 9 p.m. Pacific time. At other hours, a call goes to an adult counselor at Didi Hirsch, and a text gets no reply. You don't have to be in a crisis to reach out. Teen Line says it won't share what you say unless you are in immediate danger of hurting yourself or someone else, or someone is hurting you. Message and data rates may apply to texts.

    Call 1-800-852-8336 Text TEEN to 839863 Teen Line website

  • Trans Lifeline

    Peer support by phone, run by trans people for trans and questioning people. You don't have to be in a crisis to call, or be sure you're trans. It isn't open all day and night. It answers Monday to Friday, 10 a.m. to 6 p.m. Pacific time (1 to 9 p.m. Eastern), and closes on some holidays. Press 2 to talk with someone in Spanish. It has no text or chat service yet. It doesn't put callers on hold, so if you don't get through, try again later. Trans Lifeline says it won't call emergency services or police without your explicit request, even if you say you or someone else is in danger. It says it may still report credible threats of violence to others, and follows laws on suspected child abuse and neglect. It records calls and deletes the recordings after 90 days.

    Call 1-877-565-8860 Trans Lifeline hotline page

Support over time

A crisis line can help with a hard moment. Longer-term care can help with what is behind it. The National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) says effective treatments are available for people at risk of suicide. These include kinds of talk therapy, such as cognitive behavioral therapy and dialectical behavior therapy. The American Foundation for Suicide Prevention says recovery is possible with evidence-based treatment for depression and other mental health conditions.

Show moreShow less: Support over time
  • Talk to a health care provider. If you have a doctor or nurse you see, you can bring up your mental health yourself, and they can refer you to a specialist. NIMH has tips for that conversation.
  • Search for care near you. SAMHSA describes FindTreatment.gov as a confidential and anonymous treatment finder.
  • Call for a referral. SAMHSA's National Helpline, 1-800-662-4357, offers free treatment referrals and information, 24/7, in English and Spanish. It does not provide counseling and is not a crisis line. For Spanish, choose the option to speak with a national representative. TTY: 1-800-487-4889.

Planning for a hard moment

A safety plan is a short written list of what to do when things get hard, made ahead of time so you don't have to work it out during a crisis. A therapist or other mental health professional can help you make one. A plan does not replace professional care.

Make a safety plan, with a blank plan to print

Show moreShow less: Planning for a hard moment

NIMH says research has shown that safety plans can help lower a person's risk of acting on suicidal thoughts. Some of this research paired the plan with clinician contact and follow-up, so it doesn't show what a plan made alone would do. For children and teens, a review of brief programs built around a safety plan found no clear benefit. See what the research shows about safety plans.

A safety plan usually includes:

  • Your own warning signs that things are getting harder
  • Things you can do on your own to get through it
  • People and places that take your mind off it
  • Family or friends you can reach out to
  • Professionals and crisis lines you can contact
  • Ways to make your surroundings safer

You don't have to work through a list before asking for help. You can call or text 988, or call 911 in an emergency, at any point.

The developers of the Stanley-Brown Safety Plan offer a form (PDF) for personal use. Tidelight does not see or save what you write on it. A copy on a shared device, or on paper, could be seen by others, so keep it somewhere you feel safe.

If you're under 18

You can call or text 988 yourself, even if there is no adult you can tell. Its rules don't set a minimum age, and you don't have to give your name or age to get support. It can help to save 988 in your phone now, in case you need it later.

More for teens: reaching out, trusted adults, and privacy

Show moreShow less: If you're under 18

It can also help to tell an adult you trust, such as a parent or guardian, a relative, a teacher, a coach, a spiritual leader, or a doctor. It's okay to ask what they can keep private first.

If you tell a school counselor you are thinking about suicide, expect them to tell a parent or guardian. Their national association treats this as required, even for students who are 18. The association makes one exception. If you say you are thinking about suicide because of a parent's or guardian's abuse or neglect, the counselor must contact child protective services. A federal factsheet says caseworkers may then talk with your parents and other people around you.

Teachers and other school staff may have to pass it on too. A national model policy for school districts says all staff should report a student they believe is at risk. It says the school then tells a parent or guardian the same day or as soon as possible. The exception is when that would put the student at more risk. Rules differ by state and district.

Crisis lines keep conversations confidential, with limits. The 988 Lifeline says it may share information without your permission in some situations. That information may go to 911, police, or a mobile crisis team (a crisis team that comes to you). For example, this can happen if someone is in danger of being hurt very soon. It can also happen if a young person is being abused or neglected, as state law defines it. The Trevor Project and Crisis Text Line have similar limits. The Trevor Project says it won't tell parents or guardians unless the law or a legal order requires it.

A small number of 988 centers bill insurance and may ask for your details. You can say no and still get support. If you give them and the center bills your insurance, a parent or guardian could get a statement from the insurance company.

About Tidelight

Tidelight is an early project to help people in the United States find suicide prevention support. Tidelight is not a crisis service. The call, text, and chat links on this site connect you to outside services, such as the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. If you or someone else is in immediate danger, call 911.

Privacy: this site has no sign-ups, forms, analytics, or cookies. It is hosted on GitHub Pages, and GitHub says it logs and stores visitors' IP addresses for security. Quick exit and saved offline copies have limits. Read about privacy and what this site can't hide.
